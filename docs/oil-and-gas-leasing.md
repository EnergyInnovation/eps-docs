---
title: Increased Fuel Production from Oil and Gas Leasing
---
Where governments own land or offshore areas with oil and natural gas resources, they often make those areas available for development by leasing them to private companies, typically through competitive lease sales.  Holding more lease sales or offering more acreage can open additional areas to drilling and, over time, increase domestic production of crude oil and natural gas.  Because leased areas must be explored, permitted, and developed before they produce, any added production generally ramps up gradually over several years.  Leasing decisions involve trade-offs that policymakers and stakeholders weigh differently, including domestic energy supply, energy-sector jobs and revenue, and greenhouse gas emissions and local environmental impacts.

This policy lever represents a change in domestic crude oil and natural gas production attributable to expanded leasing.  The lever turns the change on or off; its size, by fuel and year, comes from input data (`IiFPfOaGL Increase in Fuel Production from Oil and Gas Leasing`) rather than being set by the user.

In the EPS, the additional production is assumed to be exported, so it does not change the quantity of fuel used or imported within the modeled region.  The model adds it to both the change in fuel production and the change in fuel exports (see [Change in Fuel Exports](fuels#change-in-fuel-exports) and [Components of Changes in Fuel Production and Imports](fuels#components-of-changes-in-fuel-production-and-imports) on the Fuels page).  The added exports are valued at the international market price, and the resulting revenue goes to domestic fuel suppliers, less any export tax (see [Fuel Exports and Import/Export Cash Flows](fuels#fuel-exports-and-importexport-cash-flows)).  The model also reports the CO<sub>2</sub> embedded in the additional exported fuel; because that fuel is burned outside the modeled region, its combustion emissions are not counted in the region's emissions.

The change in production also affects the rest of the model.  It changes the output of the oil and gas extraction industry in the [Industry sector](industry-ag-main), and with it that industry's own energy use and process emissions (such as methane), and it feeds the [input-output model](io-model)'s estimates of changes in jobs, employee compensation, and value added in the fossil fuel industries.
