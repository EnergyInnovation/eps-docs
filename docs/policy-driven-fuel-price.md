---
title: Policy-Driven Change in Fuel Price
---
Governments can influence fuel prices directly, not only through taxes and subsidies.  Examples include price controls or caps, tariffs set by an energy regulator, and pricing decisions by state-owned energy companies.  Such interventions may target a single fuel, or a fuel used by a particular group of consumers, such as natural gas sold to power plants or diesel sold for transportation.  This lever can also be used to explore how the energy system responds when particular fuels are more or less expensive than in the business-as-usual (BAU) case.

This policy lever directly raises or lowers the pre-tax price of a fuel, specified as a fractional change (for example, 0.1 for a 10% increase or -0.1 for a 10% decrease) for each combination of fuel and demand sector.  Because it is indexed by both fuel and sector, it can be applied broadly or narrowly, such as only to natural gas used in the electricity sector.  The change is phased in according to the policy implementation schedule and applies only to the policy case.

In the EPS, the change is applied as a multiplier to the pre-tax fuel price after the [reduction of BAU subsidies](fuels#reduction-of-bau-subsidies-for-fuels) and [fuel price deregulation](fuels#fuel-price-deregulation) adjustments; carbon and fuel taxes are then added separately (see [Policy-Driven Change in Fuel Price](fuels#policy-driven-change-in-fuel-price) on the Fuels page).  The resulting prices flow to every fuel-using sector, where they affect fuel use, technology choices, and the dispatch costs of fuel-burning power plants, in the same way as other policy-driven price changes.  Because the lever changes the price itself rather than adding a tax or subsidy, it involves no direct government payments; changes in what fuel users pay show up as changes in fuel suppliers' revenue.
